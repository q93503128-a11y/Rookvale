# World Narrative, Naming, and Story Delivery

**Status:** Core narrative-delivery philosophy and naming method locked; final cultural names, historical chronology, region names, factions, and major plot beats remain Draft

## Core narrative goal

Rookvale should feel like a place that existed before the player arrived and will continue to exist when the player walks away.

The setting is not presented through a long mandatory prologue, encyclopedia lecture, or one NPC who explains the world. Players who want only exploration and combat must be able to skip most world exposition without losing basic usability. Players who become curious should be able to reconstruct much deeper history from many small sources.

The intended model is:

```text
immediate local problem
-> ordinary NPC remarks / visual evidence / local quest
-> conflicting or incomplete accounts
-> item, building, grave, sign, ledger, ruin, enemy or boss context
-> optional deeper conversation / document / discovery
-> player inference
```

## Story is distributed across the world

No important settlement should have a single designated "lore NPC" responsible for explaining its history.

Different characters know different slices because of profession, age, family, class, faction, prejudice, education, and personal memory.

Examples of information carriers include:

- innkeepers and travelers discussing changes in traffic;
- farmers describing fields, floods, pests, taxes, or old boundaries;
- guards remembering former patrol routes;
- merchants disagreeing about why an old road or market declined;
- priests preserving ritual explanations that conflict with secular accounts;
- children repeating distorted local legends;
- veterans remembering an event differently from official memorials;
- craftspeople recognizing materials or construction methods;
- hunters noticing ecological changes before scholars do;
- graves, memorials, abandoned homes, reused masonry, graffiti, road signs, tools, ledgers, maps, songs, and damaged monuments;
- enemy placement and boss identity showing what moved into a place after people left it.

NPC dialogue should normally begin with what the NPC personally cares about, not with a history lesson.

## Multiple perspectives, not one canon lecturer

Historical truth may be distributed across several partially reliable accounts.

This does **not** mean every fact is arbitrary or unknowable. Internally, the project keeps a canonical event/history model. Individual speakers may:

- know only part of it;
- misremember dates;
- repeat propaganda;
- attribute causes incorrectly;
- omit embarrassing facts;
- exaggerate family or faction importance;
- use old regional names;
- describe the same event from a different social class or profession.

The player can often infer a more complete picture by comparing sources.

Mandatory progression must never require the player to read every optional lore fragment unless the activity is explicitly an investigation/puzzle built around that information.

## Exposition budget

Rookvale avoids long uninterrupted lore speeches by default.

A normal NPC conversation should communicate one or two useful pieces of world texture at a time. Longer optional conversations may exist when the character and subject justify them, but they should be opt-in branches rather than forced lectures.

Important background information is spaced across travel, quests, combat, visual discovery, and return visits so the player has time to digest it.

Cutscenes are reserved for events that benefit from actually being seen. They are not the default container for setting history.

## World backbone: the long retreat of maintained civilization

The current draft setting does **not** begin from a single recent magical apocalypse or a once-perfect ancient empire that suddenly vanished.

The broad historical texture is a **long civic and political retreat**.

For generations, a large part of the main landmass was tied together by overlapping systems of maintained roads, bridges, ferries, fortified passes, market towns, postal/relay houses, mines, drainage works, shrines, and underground service routes. These works were not all built by one civilization or in one era; later states repeatedly reused, extended, renamed, and misunderstood older infrastructure.

Over time, central institutions lost the money, authority, population, or political will to maintain every route. Trade shifted. Some crossings became unsafe. Local rulers and towns became more autonomous. Certain inland settlements declined while coastal or river trade grew elsewhere. Nature, monsters, bandits, local powers, and supernatural phenomena reclaimed places that had once been routine parts of everyday travel.

This decline happened unevenly and over a long enough period that some current NPCs remember busier years personally while other ruins predate living memory by centuries.

This creates several useful world layers:

1. **Living memory** — abandoned shops, closed inns, bypassed roads, veterans, changed industries, recent local grudges.
2. **Recorded history** — older wars, border changes, political unions, religious reforms, major engineering works.
3. **Deep history** — structures, names, and underground routes whose original purpose is only partially understood.

The deeper mystery of the world may eventually connect several of these layers, but the player is not told the answer at the beginning.

## Rookvale's narrative role

Rookvale is the starting Region and a place name known early, not merely the title on the box.

Its draft identity is a once-busier inland valley/crossroads whose importance came from movement through it rather than from being a grand imperial capital.

The Region contains surviving roads, river crossings, farms, woodland, older fortifications, abandoned commercial/civic sites, and routes whose importance has changed over time.

The player should be able to notice decline without an NPC stating "Rookvale declined because X" as a definitive exposition line.

Different evidence can suggest different causes: a lost bridge, shifting trade routes, political neglect, dangerous roads, ecological change, taxation, war damage, new coastal commerce, or decisions made by local elites. The canonical history may involve several causes rather than one dramatic answer.

This lets ordinary dialogue communicate history naturally: people talk about prices, travelers, empty houses, old jobs, bad roads, family memories, and local complaints rather than reciting a timeline.

## Player role

The player is not assumed to begin as a chosen savior who already matters to the whole world.

Early motivation should be local and practical: work, travel, obligation, curiosity, class advancement, money, a personal errand, or another grounded reason compatible with character-building and multiplayer.

Larger conflicts emerge because the player repeatedly encounters connected consequences across different regions.

The world should become important before the player becomes important to the world.

## Naming system

Names are designed as **cultural systems**, not isolated fantasy syllables.

Each major culture/linguistic zone receives a small naming sheet containing:

- real historical language families used only as phonological/toponymic inspiration;
- preferred consonant/vowel patterns;
- typical syllable length;
- common personal-name structures;
- common settlement/topographic elements;
- title and family-name conventions;
- sounds/letters that are uncommon;
- rules for older forms, borrowed names, and shortened everyday forms.

A full constructed language is not required. Consistency and historical layering are more important than linguistic complexity.

### Toponymic layering

A place may have more than one historical layer in its name.

Examples of allowed patterns:

- an old local name retained after conquest or migration;
- a newer trade-language translation of an older geographic name;
- a settlement whose spoken name shortened over centuries;
- a fortress named by one culture while the surrounding river keeps an older name;
- two neighboring groups using different names for the same pass;
- a modern map preserving an obsolete administrative label locals rarely use.

This allows names themselves to communicate history.

### Readability rules

Rookvale avoids:

- random apostrophes and punctuation used only to look exotic;
- every fantasy place being a compound of dramatic nouns such as `Shadow + Fang + Citadel`;
- excessively long names that players cannot remember or say;
- naming every culture with the same pseudo-English or pseudo-Latin sound;
- copying real-world words/names wholesale when a transformed phonological reference will do;
- naming every dungeon after its gameplay role rather than the people who actually used or feared the place.

Short, pronounceable, memorable names are preferred for frequently visited places.

`Rookvale` is retained as an established name and becomes one anchor for the naming family of its surrounding culture; the rest of that naming family will be built deliberately rather than generated ad hoc.

## Map and level storytelling

Map layout is part of narrative design.

Settlements, industries, roads, and ruins must have plausible relationships to terrain and livelihood.

Examples:

- fishing settlements belong on useful water, not arbitrary empty tiles;
- mining communities sit near actual extraction routes and spoil/transport infrastructure;
- forts control something worth controlling: a pass, bridge, harbor, road, boundary, mine, or water source;
- market towns form around routes and hinterlands that can sustain them;
- abandoned structures should still reveal what they were for through layout and remains;
- later construction may reuse older walls, foundations, tunnels, or stones.

Points of interest should be visually/structurally legible enough that exploration is intentional rather than systematic wall-hugging.

Major routes should repeatedly offer visible detours, landmarks, silhouettes, changes in terrain, or local clues that encourage voluntary exploration.

## Environmental narrative vocabulary

Worldbuilding can be delivered through:

- architecture and construction layers;
- road quality and abandonment;
- changed river courses and broken crossings;
- fields returning to scrub;
- abandoned workshops and tools;
- old defensive lines now facing the "wrong" direction because borders changed;
- reused temples, houses, forts, mines, and civic buildings;
- local monster nests occupying human-made structures for understandable reasons;
- burial practices;
- clothing and equipment differences;
- food, crops, livestock, and trade goods;
- graffiti, signs, notices, tally marks, carvings, memorials;
- short item descriptions where appropriate;
- boss arenas that reveal what the place used to be.

Environmental evidence should often support, complicate, or contradict NPC claims.

## Optional lore support

The game may keep a concise player-facing record of discovered people, places, rumors, and unresolved clues, but it should not automatically turn every discovery into a huge encyclopedia article.

A future journal can distinguish:

- **Known** — directly observed or broadly established;
- **Claimed** — what an NPC/source says;
- **Rumored** — unverified lead;
- **Resolved** — enough evidence has been found to clarify the subject.

This is a candidate UX direction, not yet a locked interface design.

## Research-derived design lessons

- FromSoftware's subtle narrative works because players receive hints from items, side characters, and environments and are allowed to interpret them rather than being automatically told the whole story.
- Disco Elysium demonstrates that world knowledge can arrive as snippets from characters with different worldviews rather than as one neutral manual.
- Outer Wilds demonstrates a clue network where discoveries point to other locations and shows the importance of spacing narrative pieces so the player can digest them.
- Pillars of Eternity demonstrates the usefulness of culture-specific naming/language systems rooted in real linguistic references rather than random fantasy syllables.
- The Witcher 3 demonstrates placing settlements and occupations according to terrain/economy, and giving villages/locations distinctive local stories.
- Xenoblade demonstrates route design where the main path repeatedly presents enticing detours that remain convenient to rejoin.

These are structural lessons only. Rookvale does not copy their setting, names, plots, factions, exact dialogue, quests, or lore.
