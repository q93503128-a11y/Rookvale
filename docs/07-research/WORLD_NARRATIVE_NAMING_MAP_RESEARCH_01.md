# World Narrative, Naming, and Map-Design Research 01

**Date:** 2026-10-16

**Purpose:** Research external approaches to indirect worldbuilding, fragmented/optional lore, culturally coherent naming, and exploration-map construction before locking Rookvale's narrative and naming rules.

## References retained

### Dark Souls / Elden Ring — indirect interpretation

Hidetaka Miyazaki has described subtle/environmental storytelling as a way to let players discover hints through items and side characters and interpret the world's story themselves rather than have the game automatically tell it to them.

**Rookvale lesson:** important history can be reconstructed from many small observations. Do not require every player to consume deep lore.

**Do not copy:** opaque quest logic, exact Souls-style item-description density, intentionally cryptic NPC behavior, setting mythology, or terminology.

### Disco Elysium — snippets and viewpoints

ZA/UM writers have discussed world information arriving in fragments rather than a clean universal manual, and characters representing different ideas/worldviews. The relatively compact map gains depth through repeated contact with people and overlapping local stories.

**Rookvale lesson:** different NPCs should know different things and disagree for understandable reasons. Background information should emerge from work, family, profession, class, politics, age, and local prejudice.

**Do not copy:** political framework, voice, prose style, skill-dialogue system, Revachol/Martinaise setting, or character archetypes.

### Outer Wilds — clue network and information spacing

Mobius Digital describes its narrative as embedded pieces discovered across locations, with discoveries acting as clues pointing toward other places and larger mysteries. Their paper-prototype testing also showed that concentrating too much lore in one place overwhelmed players and that downtime between discoveries was useful.

The team also emphasized visual guidance so players can make intentional exploration choices without checking every corner.

**Rookvale lesson:** spread history across places and people; let discoveries create new leads; avoid a single lore dump; make POIs legible enough that exploration is curiosity-driven rather than exhaustive wall-hugging.

**Do not copy:** time loop, Nomai, rumor-map structure wholesale, planet puzzle design, or archaeology premise.

### Pillars of Eternity — naming as a linguistic/cultural system

Josh Sawyer has described Pillars' constructed languages as being based on real-world linguistic references, with different cultures drawing from different language families. He has also emphasized focusing worldbuilding on what matters in the current region rather than explaining the entire universe at once.

**Rookvale lesson:** build compact naming sheets per culture using historical phonological/toponymic references. Names should reveal cultural and historical layering instead of being random fantasy syllables.

**Do not copy:** Eora cultures, names, exact language mappings, colonial history, or constructed-language vocabulary.

### The Witcher 3 — geography, livelihood, and local stories

CD Projekt Red environment/quest designers described placing settlements according to believable terrain and livelihood: fishing communities near water, hunters/lumber activity near forests, etc. They also sought density/variety and memorable local stories rather than relying only on the main quest.

**Rookvale lesson:** economy and settlement form must follow geography. Every village/route should have a reason to exist and at least one memorable local circumstance.

**Do not copy:** Witcher setting, Slavic names, monster contracts, quest plots, or White Orchard/Velen structure.

### Xenoblade Chronicles — detours that rejoin naturally

Nintendo's Iwata Asks interview describes fields designed so the route toward the story objective repeatedly presents places players want to detour into, without making it painful to rejoin the main route afterward.

**Rookvale lesson:** major paths should expose tempting side routes and landmarks; exploration must not punish the player with tedious backtracking.

**Do not copy:** field scale, world-on-titans concept, collectibles, or quest structure.

### CrossCode — content-first area/town/dungeon production

Radical Fish described building and polishing gameplay areas, towns, dungeons, and optional quests before finishing much of the main story.

**Rookvale lesson:** the world should work as a place and gameplay space before the global narrative is layered over it. Do not make map geography exist only to serve exposition.

**Do not copy:** MMO-inside-a-game setting, elemental dungeon pattern, puzzle language, or exact progression format.

## Naming synthesis

The strongest retained approach is not to invent one 'fantasy language', but to create several compact naming families.

Each family should specify:

1. one or more historical language families used as phonological inspiration;
2. sound inventory tendencies;
3. preferred syllable shapes and word lengths;
4. settlement/topographic suffix/prefix behavior;
5. personal/family/title structure;
6. historical borrowings and old forms;
7. common shortened colloquial forms;
8. explicit anti-patterns.

Place names should also be allowed to preserve older linguistic layers after conquest, migration, translation, economic change, or political renaming.

## Narrative synthesis

Rookvale should maintain an internal canonical history but expose it through subjective sources.

A useful information ladder:

```text
what is happening here now
-> who is affected
-> how locals explain it
-> what physical evidence shows
-> how older records/people disagree
-> what larger pattern the player can infer
```

The setting should support players who skip nearly all optional conversation while rewarding those who compare fragments.

## Map synthesis

The map should be designed simultaneously as:

- traversal space;
- economic geography;
- combat ecology;
- visual composition;
- narrative evidence.

A location is suspicious if it cannot answer questions such as:

- Why was this settlement built here?
- What do people here eat, make, move, protect, or extract?
- Why does this road exist?
- What did this ruin used to do?
- Why is this enemy/boss here rather than somewhere else?
- What can the player see that makes them want to leave the main road?
- How does the detour return or open a shortcut?

## Rookvale-specific implication

The starting Region should communicate historical decline through ordinary life rather than an opening narration. Empty commercial buildings, changed traffic patterns, reused infrastructure, old route markers, local complaints, family memories, and occupied ruins can establish that Rookvale was once busier.

The cause should initially remain multi-causal and disputed. That allows later content to reveal a deeper history without reducing the Region to one exposition sentence.
